Basement Waterproofing From the Inside in Derry, NH

Interior waterproofing gathers water that has already entered and sends it out. This guide explains how it works, and where it stops.

By the Derry Express Plumbing team. Published October 22, 2026. Updated October 22, 2026.

Call (603) 815-9179

The short answer: interior systems manage water, they do not stop it

Interior waterproofing does not keep water out of the soil around your foundation. It gives water that reaches the wall or floor a path to a sump pit, where a pump sends it outside. That is a drainage system, not a seal, and it works well when the water is groundwater.

The main alternative is exterior waterproofing, which digs down along the wall and seals it. That is more thorough and much more disruptive, so many owners try the interior route first. The right choice depends on how the water enters and what the budget allows.

What an interior drain installation involves

The sequence is similar from house to house.

  1. Open the floor along the wall. A strip of concrete is cut and removed around the perimeter where water enters.
  2. Lay drain pipe in gravel. Perforated pipe sits in stone and slopes toward a pit.
  3. Install the sump pit and pump. The pit collects water, and the pump discharges outdoors.
  4. Patch the floor and test. New concrete is poured, and the system is tested with water.

Cheaper steps to take first

Before you open the floor, check the basics. Clean gutters, extend downspouts at least several feet from the foundation, and regrade the soil so it slopes away from the house. Seal window wells and keep them clear of debris. These steps can solve the problem, and they cost little.

If the cellar still takes on water, an interior drain is a reasonable next step. A battery backup for the pump protects you during storm outages, as explained on our sump pump installation and repair page.

Questions for any contractor

Ask where the water will go, how the pump will be powered during an outage, what warranty applies and what voids it. Ask how many similar jobs they have done on houses like yours. Be wary of a contractor who recommends the most expensive system without testing for a plumbing leak first.

How the drain and pit work together

Water that enters a basement tends to appear where the wall meets the floor, since that joint is the weakest point in a poured foundation. A drain channel at the edge catches the water before it spreads. The channel slopes toward a sump pit, which collects the flow so the pump can lift it outdoors.

The system depends on the pump and on power. A pump that fails or an outage during a storm leaves the drain full and the water rising. That is why a backup pump or a battery system belongs in any plan, and why a high-water alarm is cheap insurance.

Maintenance is simple. Check the pit each season, clear any silt, test the float and watch that the discharge runs freely. Gunk in the pit shortens pump life, and a sticky float is the usual reason pumps fail on the day they are needed.

What interior work cannot do

Interior drainage does not repair a failing wall. If a foundation is bowing, sinking or cracking in a way that suggests movement, a drain only manages the water, and a structural engineer should look at the cause. Water in the wall also leaves salts on the surface, which a drain does not remove.

Interior systems also do not control humidity. A cellar can be dry underfoot and still feel damp in summer. A dehumidifier and ventilation handle that problem, and they work better once liquid water is under control.

Planning the project around the seasons

Interior drainage work is easier when the ground is not frozen and the cellar is dry enough to cut the floor. Late summer and early fall are common choices. Spring is when the problem shows itself, but contractors are busiest then, so booking in the quiet months can save waiting.

Ask how the contractor protects belongings during the job. Cutting concrete throws dust, and the work area needs sheeting and a clear path for equipment. Move stored items out of the cellar before the plumber arrives, and keep the water heater and furnace accessible.

A realistic expectation for results

A well-designed interior system keeps the floor dry in most storms, but it will not stop all dampness. Walls may still show salt stains, and humidity can remain high in summer. Set expectations before the work starts, so a dry floor with a musty smell does not feel like a failed job.

Ask the contractor to describe what a normal result looks like, and agree on how to judge it after the first heavy rain.

Derry notes for interior drainage

Rockingham County has flood or storm declarations on record for May 2006, April 2007, July 2008 and March 2010, plus Tropical Storm Irene in 2011. The regional planning commission lists them as county events, so treat them as a reminder that heavy rain years happen.

Derry's code enforcement office at 603-432-6148 can tell you whether a permit applies to a new sump pit or a drain connection. Sump discharge should go outdoors, and connecting it to a sanitary sewer is generally not allowed, so confirm with the town.
