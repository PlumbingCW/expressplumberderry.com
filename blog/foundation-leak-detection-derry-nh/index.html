Foundation Leak Detection in Derry, NH: Pipe or Groundwater?

Wet concrete does not tell you where the water came from. This guide shows how to sort pipe leaks from groundwater before anyone digs.

By the Derry Express Plumbing team. Published October 17, 2026. Updated October 17, 2026.

Call (603) 815-9179

The short answer: test the meter before you test the soil

Water at a foundation comes from one of three places. It can be a leaking supply or drain pipe, groundwater pushing through the wall or floor, or surface water that grading and gutters failed to move away. A plumbing leak runs whether it has rained or not, while groundwater and surface water follow the weather.

That difference gives you a simple first test. If the cellar is wet during a dry week, suspect a pipe. If it is wet only after storms or snowmelt, suspect drainage. The water meter then confirms or rules out a supply leak in fifteen minutes.

The meter test for a supply leak

You need a dry stretch and the ability to stop all water use in the house.

  1. Stop all water use. Make sure nobody is running a tap, a washer, an ice maker or a humidifier.
  2. Read and photograph the meter. Note the leak indicator, if it has one, and the digits.
  3. Wait fifteen minutes. Use no water at all.
  4. Read again. Any movement means water is flowing somewhere. Call the plumber and give the readings.

What the plumber does with the tools

After the meter test, the plumber pressure tests the supply lines, listens with electronic equipment and checks the drain lines with a camera. Thermal imaging can reveal a warm line under a slab. Moisture meters show how far the water has spread.

These steps narrow the search before anything is opened. If the leak is in the supply line under the slab, the repair may be a reroute through the walls instead of breaking up the floor. Our leak detection page describes the process, and the basement waterproofing page covers groundwater.

When it is groundwater

If the meter does not move and the cellar is wet after storms, the water is coming from outside. The cheapest fixes come first. Clear the gutters, extend the downspouts, regrade the soil so it slopes away and seal window wells. If water still arrives, an interior drain along the wall and a sump pit and pump manage what is left.

Do not pay for interior sealing paint or injection alone to stop active seepage, because the water pressure outside will defeat a thin coating. Ask the contractor how the water will leave the house, not only how the crack will be covered.

Signs that a leak is under the slab

A leak under a concrete slab may show as a damp or warm patch on the floor, a hissing sound at night or a meter that keeps turning. Carpet over a slab can grow musty, and baseboards can swell. A hot water line leak under a slab can make part of the floor feel warm underfoot.

These signs deserve a test, not a guess. Slab leak repair can mean breaking the floor, rerouting the line through walls or attic space, or relining the pipe. The plumber explains each approach once the location is confirmed, and you see the price before work begins.

Gutters, grading and window wells: the quiet culprits

A surprising amount of foundation water begins on the roof. A clogged gutter spills over at the edge, and the water lands against the foundation. A downspout that ends beside the wall pours hundreds of gallons into the soil during a storm.

Grading matters as much as gutters. Soil should slope away from the foundation for several feet, and window wells should drain freely. Fixing these basics is inexpensive compared with interior systems, and they often change a wet cellar into a dry one.

Documenting the problem for a plumber

Take dated photos of wet areas, note the weather on the days the water appears and record the meter readings from the test. Mark the edge of the damp area with tape so the plumber can see whether it grows. Notes like these save time on the first visit.

If you suspect the supply line, say so when you call. If you suspect drainage, mention which walls get wet first.
