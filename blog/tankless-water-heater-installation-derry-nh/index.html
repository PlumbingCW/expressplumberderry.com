Tankless Water Heater Installation in Derry, NH: What to Know

A tankless heater saves floor space and removes the tank, but the house has to be ready for it. This guide covers what to check before you buy.

By the Derry Express Plumbing team. Published October 6, 2026. Updated October 6, 2026.

Call (603) 815-9179

The short answer: tankless installs are a house upgrade, not a swap

A tankless water heater heats water on demand with a burner or electric elements, so it needs far more fuel or power at one moment than a tank does. That is why a tankless install is often a gas line, venting or panel project as much as a plumbing one. The plumber looks at those three things before quoting.

If your existing heater is a gas tank in a closet, the new unit may need a larger gas line, a different vent and a place on the wall. If it is an electric tank, the unit may need heavy circuits that your panel cannot supply. Ask for these items in writing, so the quote is complete.

What happens on install day

A typical visit follows a set order, and the plumber explains each step as it happens.

  1. Shut off and drain the old tank. Water and fuel are closed, and the old heater is drained and removed.
  2. Mount the unit and run vent and fuel lines. The plumber follows the manufacturer's instructions for clearances and materials.
  3. Connect water with isolation valves. Valves let the unit be flushed later without cutting pipes.
  4. Commission and test. The plumber tests ignition, temperature and flow, and shows you the controls.

Size by flow, not by family size

A tankless unit is rated by how many gallons per minute it can heat at a given temperature rise. In a cold New Hampshire winter, incoming water is colder, so the same unit delivers less hot water than it would in summer. That is why sizing starts with the number of fixtures that run at once and the coldest water temperature you expect.

A household that showers in the morning while the dishwasher runs needs more capacity than the same household using one fixture at a time. Some homes use two smaller units. The plumber adds up your peak demand and picks a model with some margin.

Gas, electric or condensing: how the choice plays out

Gas tankless units come in two broad types. Standard models vent hot exhaust and can often use a single-wall or stainless vent, while condensing models extract more heat and produce acidic condensate that needs a drain and a neutralizer. Both need combustion air and a sealed vent path, so the install location matters as much as the model you choose.

Electric tankless units avoid venting entirely, which makes them easy to place, but they pull heavy current. A whole-house electric unit can ask for several large breakers, and many older panels cannot supply that without an upgrade. Smaller electric units suit a single bathroom or a point-of-use job, which is why panel capacity is the first question the plumber asks.

Whichever fuel you choose, the unit needs access for service. Leave clear space in front for the panel and below for the isolation valves. A tankless heater squeezed into a tight closet is harder to flush, harder to repair and more likely to be neglected.

Winter performance in a New Hampshire house

Incoming water temperature changes with the seasons. In a cold New Hampshire winter the supply can be much colder than in summer, so the heater must raise the temperature further at the same flow. The result is that a unit that handles two showers in July may handle only one in January.

Plan for the worst month, not the best. Ask the plumber to size the unit for winter flow, and consider a model with a higher capacity than the minimum. If the household is large, two units or a unit plus a small tank can be a better answer than one unit stretched to its limit.

If you are on town water, ask what the department reports about your supply before deciding on scale protection.
