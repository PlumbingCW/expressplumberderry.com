Double Sink Garbage Disposal Plumbing in Derry, NH

Two bowls and one disposal means one drain has to serve both. This guide explains how the pieces connect and where the setup goes wrong.

By the Derry Express Plumbing team. Published October 8, 2026. Updated October 8, 2026.

Call (603) 815-9179

The short answer: one bowl feeds the disposal and the other joins it

On a typical double sink, one bowl drains straight into the disposal. The other bowl drains through a tailpiece and a short horizontal pipe, which connects to the disposal's side outlet or to a branch fitting just beyond it. Everything then flows through a single trap and into the wall drain.

That layout is simple, but it has several joints that can leak. A wrong slope on the horizontal pipe also causes standing water in one bowl, which people often blame on the disposal. Most double-sink problems come down to alignment, slope and a missed connection.

How the parts connect

Follow the water from the sink to the wall.

  1. Bowl one: flange and disposal. A mounting assembly seals the sink opening and holds the disposal underneath.
  2. Bowl two: strainer and tailpiece. A drain assembly drops into a tailpiece that runs toward the disposal discharge.
  3. Discharge elbow and trap arm. The disposal outlet and the second bowl's pipe join, then pass through a P-trap.
  4. Dishwasher drain. A hose from the dishwasher attaches to a nipple on the disposal, after the knockout plug is removed.

The knockout plug that stops dishwashers

A new disposal comes with a plastic plug inside the dishwasher inlet. If the dishwasher is connected without removing it, the machine cannot drain and water backs up into the dishwasher tub. People often think the dishwasher has failed.

Remove the plug with a screwdriver and a hammer before connecting the hose, and take the plug out of the unit so it does not clog the grinder. The hose should loop high under the counter, or run through an air gap, so that sink water cannot flow backward.

Choosing a disposal for a two-bowl sink

Horsepower, feed type and sound insulation are the main choices. A continuous-feed unit runs while you push scraps in, and a batch-feed unit runs only when the stopper is turned. Larger motors resist jams better, and models with insulation run quieter.

Check the sink first. A disposal needs a standard 3.5 to 4.5 inch drain opening, and some older or specialty sinks need an adapter. The plumber confirms fit and clearance under the sink, since a deep disposal may hit the trap on a shallow cabinet.

Installing a disposal on a double sink: the order that works

Start by shutting off power at the breaker, since a disposal is wired or plugged in beneath the sink. Remove the old strainer or the old disposal, clean the opening and set the new mounting flange on a bead of plumber's putty. Tighten it from below until the putty squeezes out evenly and wipe the excess away.

Hang the disposal on its mounting ring and rotate it until the discharge outlet lines up with the drain pipe. Connect the second bowl's tailpiece to the same drain with a continuous waste fitting. Check that the horizontal run slopes gently toward the wall so water does not stand in the pipe.

Last, connect the dishwasher hose, the P-trap and the power. Fill both bowls and release them together while you watch every joint. A slow weep at a slip nut shows up under load, so run the test before you close the cabinet.

Noise, smell and upkeep

A disposal that growls or rattles may have a utensil in the chamber, and a unit that runs but hums needs the jam cleared. Smells come from food film on the shredder ring and the underside of the splash guard. Scrub them with a brush and dish soap, then run cold water to flush the chamber.

Citrus peels and ice are common suggestions for freshening a disposal, but they do not fix buildup. Regular cleaning and cold water do more. If the drain line is the source of the odor, a drain cleaning visit clears the line behind the trap.
